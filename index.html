Openings

I have often felt a motion of love to leave some hints in writing of my experience of the goodness of God; and now, in the thirty-sixth year of my age, I begin this work. John Woolman, opening his journal, 1756

Friends have kept journals for three and a half centuries — not diaries of events, but a record of what was opened to a person and what they did about it. George Fox called such a moment an opening. Woolman began his at thirty-six and wrote in it until the smallpox took him in York. What they wrote down was mostly plain: where they went, whom they sat with, what troubled them, where they were faithful and where they were not.

This book works the same way. It gives you a query at whatever rhythm you set, and a blank page. You are not answering the query for anyone. You are letting it work on you, in writing, over time — which is slower than thinking and harder to fool.

The queries come from the Advices and Queries of Iowa Yearly Meeting (Conservative) and from the writings on the shelf inside: Woolman, Fox, Penington, Kelly, Faith and Practice, and several living voices. You can ask the shelf a question at any time, and you can ask for queries on what you have just written — never advice, and never a verdict, in the manner of a clearness committee.

The practice

how Friends have used a journal, and how to use this one

what a Quaker journal is

It is not a diary of what happened. It is a record of leadings — of what was opened to you, what you did about it, and what it cost. Woolman's journal spends more words on his own reluctance than on his travels. James Parnell was dead at nineteen and had already written. The point is faithfulness, recorded plainly enough that you cannot later pretend otherwise.

plainness

Write it plain. No performance, no audience, no attempt to sound like a Friend. The Advices ask us to speak "clearly, simply and from our own experience" in meeting; the same holds on the page.

the query

A query is not a question with an answer. Faith and Practice "suggests rather than commands, and raises questions or queries rather than giving specific answers." Carry one for the whole period. Come back to it. Notice when your answer changes.

where it goes wrong

Two ways, mainly. Writing only when moved, so the record has nothing in it but the high days. And writing to a reader — even an imagined one — which turns the journal into a defence. If you find yourself explaining, stop and write the thing you were explaining around.

asking

The shelf holds real passages from the sources named on it, carried inside this page. When you ask it something, it answers from those passages only, and names which. It cannot reach the full books. When it does not know, it says so.
